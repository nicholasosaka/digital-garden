The 2010s [push for STEM education](https://www.congress.gov/bill/114th-congress/house-bill/1020) saw high schools introduce [computer science education](https://www.npr.org/sections/ed/2016/01/14/462954645/adding-beauty-and-joy-to-obamas-push-for-computer-science-teaching) into their curricula. This in and of itself isn't a problem, indeed it's a good thing that students be introduced to topics such as algorithms or statistics during their secondary education. However, coupled with the instrumentalization of higher education, where college is increasingly seen as job training, this laid the ground work for the stemification of writing.

During the early months of accessible and commercialized large language models (LLM), the phrase "[prompt engineering](https://cloud.google.com/discover/what-is-prompt-engineering)" came into the limelight. This term was used primarily to refer to the skill of providing suitable (or effective?) text such that an LLM could predict text following the "prompt" that would deliver usable results. In practice, this means providing clear (and simple) instructions, maybe an example, some context on the problem at hand, and parameters for formatting.

To be clear, these are just tips for good technical writing, a common subfield or concentration within English majors. What's happening here is that the field of artificial intelligence (or maybe just the field of large language model inference!) has taken well established principles from another discipline, relabeled it, and passed it off as something original. This is done by "STEMifiing" writing practices. Giving examples? That's multi-shot prompting. Building up to a complex concept through intermediate ones? That's Chain of Thought prompting. Those terms are taken straight from the Google Cloud topic guide for prompt engineering, linked earlier.

The practice of *good writing* wasn't seen as a valuable skill until it could be maneuvered into the STEM amalgamation as something that could be useful for the AI hype train.
